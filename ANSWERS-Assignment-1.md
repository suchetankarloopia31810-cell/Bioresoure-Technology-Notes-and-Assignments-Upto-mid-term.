## Q1. Objective of the course "Bioresource Technology"

The aim of the course is to **provide fundamental knowledge for bioenergy generation and product formation** with the help of various **conversion processes** that are suited to the diverse characteristics of bioresources.

In short, it teaches how to convert non-fossil, biogenic materials (biomass, wastes) into:
- **Energy** (heat, power, transport fuels), and
- **Useful products** (chemicals, materials),

using biochemical, thermo-chemical and physico-chemical routes, in a safe, sustainable and profitable manner.

## Q3. "Bioresource Technology" and recent trends

**Definition:** Bioresource Technology is an **interdisciplinary area that integrates engineering design with biological sciences.** Engineering principles are applied for the proper utilization, enhancement and sustainability of the world's natural (non-fossil, biogenic) resources in a safe and profitable manner. It seeks solutions to societal problems through the involvement of plants, animals, microbes and the environment.

It is similar to biotechnology, except that it is based on **agricultural and biological feedstocks**, and it encompasses a wider range of elements: biomass, biological waste treatment, biofuels and biorefineries, microbial products, and physico-/thermo-chemical processes for biomass and bioprocesses.

**Recent trends:**
- Biofuels and biorefineries (biodiesel, bioethanol, biobutanol, biogas, biohydrogen, biofuels from algae).
- Microbial fuel cells, life-cycle assessment and energy/green-house balances of biofuels.
- Advanced thermochemical conversion (gasification → syngas, fast pyrolysis → bio-oil, hydrothermal conversion).
- Microbial products (biofertilizers, biopesticides, biopolymers, enzymes, organic acids).
- Bioremediation, phytoremediation, solid-waste management and effluent biotreatment.

## Q4. Challenges and opportunities in Bioresource Technology

**Opportunities**
- Continuous depletion of fossil fuels and rising petroleum prices make renewable bioresources attractive.
- Biomass is **abundant, evenly distributed, renewable, biodegradable, carbon-neutral and benign** for carbon sequestration.
- Dual benefit: **clean environment + cheaper energy** (especially from waste).
- Wide product range — energy, transport fuels and chemical feedstocks.
- High national energy targets (India: 175 GW by 2022, 275 GW by 2026–27) create demand.

**Challenges**
- Low energy density, high/variable moisture, seasonal availability and dispersed collection of biomass.
- Cost of conversion (e.g., bio-diesel is still not fully cost-competitive with fossil fuels).
- Need for pretreatment, drying and densification.
- Technological immaturity of some routes (lignocellulosic hydrolysis, bio-oil upgrading).
- Competition with food security and other renewables (wind, solar).

## Q5. Define, explain and classify "Bioresources"

**Definition:** Bioresources are **non-fossil, biogenic resources** that can be used by humans for multiple purposes — to produce **food**, **substantial products**, and/or as an **energy carrier**. They include agriculture, forestry and biologically derived wastes. Bioproducts (antibiotics, single-cell protein, alcohol) and bioenergy sources (biodiesel, syngas, biochar) are derived from them, and they provide an eco-friendly, sustainable environment.

**Two major groups:**
- **Natural bioresources** – obtained directly from nature: plants (biomass), animals (fats), microbes (algae).
- **Anthropogenic bioresources** – arise through human activity: rice husk, bagasse, wheat straw, etc.

**Classification (by stage of generation):**
1. **Primary** – generated for a specific purpose through forests/agriculture/aquaculture (grain, fish, potato, bamboo, algae).
2. **Secondary** – generated during processing of primary bioresources / industrial by-products, produced in large amounts, low impurities (molasses, bagasse, corncob).
3. **Tertiary** – residues separated along the processing chain; small amounts, lower value, degrade on storage.
4. **Quaternary** – generated **after a product has been used**; categorised by time delay:
   - *Short term* (hours): animal faeces, excreta.
   - *Mid term* (days–months): packaging, newsprint.
   - *Long term* (years–centuries): wood construction material, furniture.

## Q6. Areas of activities of Bioresource Technology

1. **Biological waste treatment** – activated sludge; aerobic & anaerobic digestion; biodegradation of toxic compounds; biofiltration; biological nutrient removal; bioremediation; biosorption of heavy metals; phytoremediation; effluent treatment (food, dye, textile); solid-waste management.
2. **Biofuels and biorefineries** – biodiesel, bioethanol, biobutanol, biogas, biohydrogen, algal biofuels; microbial fuel cells; LCA and energy/GHG balances; modelling & design.
3. **Biomass as feedstock** – enzymatic conversion of cellulose/lignocellulose; composting; enzymatic pulping; municipal waste; vermicomposting.
4. **Bioprocessing** – algal harvesting, biocatalysis, bioleaching; bioreactor design & operation; kinetics, modelling, optimization; solid-state & submerged fermentation.
5. **Microbial products** – biofertilizers, biopesticides, biopolymers, biosurfactants, enzymes, mushrooms, organic acids, metabolites.
6. **Physico-/thermo-chemical processes for biomass** – catalysis, combustion, gasification, pyrolysis, hydrothermal conversion, pretreatment.

## Q7. Significance of bioresources in healthcare — Indian perspective

- The Indian subcontinent is rich in **forest diversity** and ancient healthcare traditions — **Ayurveda / Hamdard** ("science of life"), which provide both **curative** and **preventive** drugs.
- A Sanskrit shloka states: *"There is no plant on Earth which does not have medicinal properties."*
- Population growth and industrialisation have invited newer diseases; modern **synthetic drugs** are not easily digested, have **side effects**, and their manufacture causes **air and water pollution**.
- Hence people increasingly prefer **bioresource-based (herbal) medicines** to stay healthy and avoid side effects.
- Herbs are natural; their chemical composition varies with growing conditions, so quality control and cultivation are needed; many **NGOs** now cultivate medicinal plants and botanical gardens.
- The subcontinent has the **richest area of medicinal plants**; there is an urgent need to establish the value of these traditional bioresources in both **national and international** perspectives, with WHO working on guidelines for traditional medicine.

## Q11(a). What is biomass and its significance as a bioresource

**Biomass** is all **organic matter generated through photosynthesis**, including matter produced by land- and water-based vegetation. It is a **renewable, biodegradable** energy source whose ultimate origin is the **sun** (plants capture solar energy during photosynthesis; <1 % of available sunlight is stored as chemical energy). It is composed of **cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin and extractives**. Stored chemical energy is released when C–C, C–H, C=O bonds are broken by digestion, combustion or decomposition.

*Examples:* agricultural & agro-industrial wastes/residues, energy plantations, farm wastes, forest products and wastes, animal waste, aquatic biomass. (In the Indian context, animal waste/cattle dung is treated as a separate category, not part of biomass.)

**Significance:**
- Alternative to depleting fossil fuels; addresses rising prices and energy demand.
- Biodegradable, environmentally friendly, sustainable → primary candidate for renewable energy.
- Most abundant renewable source, evenly distributed worldwide; can substitute fossil fuels in power plants.
- "Stored solar energy," **carbon-neutral, non-toxic**, benign for carbon sequestration.
- Low ash, nitrogen and sulfur content → environmentally attractive.

## Q11(b). Important properties of biomass for commercial application

The properties affecting conversion technology and cost are:

1. **Moisture content** – intrinsic (weather-independent) + extrinsic (weather-dependent) moisture. High moisture suits **biochemical** conversion (anaerobic digestion); **thermochemical** routes need low moisture. Water is non-combustible, lowers heating value and the overall energy balance, and affects pyrolysis products (though some moisture aids H₂ production in gasification).
2. **Fixed carbon (FC) & volatile matter (VM)** – the two stores of chemical energy in solid fuels. FC = mass remaining after volatiles are released (excluding ash & moisture); VM = portion driven off as gas on heating (to 950 °C, 7 min). They indicate the ease of ignition and gasification/oxidation. Determined by **proximate analysis** (VM, ash, moisture, FC).
3. **Heating value (HV)** – energy released on combustion in air. **HHV (gross)** includes latent heat of water vapour (maximum recoverable energy); **LHV (net)** excludes it (the practically usable value). Correlations exist based on both proximate and ultimate analysis.
4. **Ash / residue content** – inorganic residue after combustion (oxides of Si, Fe, K, Mg, Ca, Al). Acts as a heat sink, lowers efficiency, can form **slag**, and raises handling/processing cost. In biochemical conversion the solid residue (recalcitrant carbon) exceeds the ash content.
5. **Elemental composition (C, H, O, N, S)** – the **ultimate analysis**. Higher O/C and H/C ratios (see Van Krevelen diagram) **lower** the energy value because C–O and C–H bonds hold less energy than C–C bonds.
6. **Alkali metal content** and **cellulose/lignin ratio** – influence ash behaviour/slagging and the conversion route.

## Q12. Mathematical model: HHV vs Lignin content

Fitting a **linear regression** of HHV (db, MJ/kg) against lignin L (wt%, db) for the 9 biomass samples gives:

> **HHV (MJ/kg) = 0.0979 × L + 16.29**

(The lecture-notes fit on a sub-set gives the equivalent line HHV = 0.0977 L + 16.31.)

Interpretation: HHV **increases linearly with lignin content** — lignin is the most energy-dense and most reduced (lowest O/C) component of biomass, so biomass richer in lignin has a higher heating value.
