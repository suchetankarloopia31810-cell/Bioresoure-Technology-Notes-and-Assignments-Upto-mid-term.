## Q1. Biomass combustion fundamentals + basic chemical equations + two-stage combustion

**Combustion** is the **burning of biomass in air** to convert the chemical energy stored in biomass into **heat, mechanical power or electricity**, using equipment such as stoves, furnaces, boilers, steam turbines and turbo-generators. It produces hot gases at **≈ 800–1000 °C**. It is feasible for biomass with **moisture < 50 %** (otherwise pre-drying is required); high-moisture biomass is better suited to biological conversion. Net conversion efficiency of biomass combustion power plants is **≈ 20–40 %**.

**Basic chemical equations** (complete oxidation of the fuel elements):

- C + O₂ → CO₂
- H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O
- S + O₂ → SO₂
- (incomplete: C + ½O₂ → CO)

**Two-stage combustion (primary + secondary air)** — flow description:

```
            BIOMASS FUEL
                 │
                 ▼
   ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │  PRIMARY ZONE (sub-stoich)│  ← PRIMARY AIR (under-stoichiometric)
   │  Drying → Devolatilisation│     • drying, pyrolysis/devolatilisation
   │  → partial oxidation/char │     • produces CO, H2, CH4, volatiles, char
   └──────────────┬───────────┘
                  │  combustible gases (CO, H2, CxHy)
                  ▼
   ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │ SECONDARY ZONE (gas phase)│  ← SECONDARY AIR (excess air)
   │  Complete oxidation       │     • CO+½O2→CO2 , H2+½O2→H2O
   └──────────────┬───────────┘
                  ▼
        FLUE GAS (CO2, H2O, N2, O2) + ASH + HEAT
```

- **Stage 1 (primary air, sub-stoichiometric):** drying and **devolatilisation/pyrolysis** of the solid, plus partial oxidation of char — releases volatile combustible gases (CO, H₂, light hydrocarbons).
- **Stage 2 (secondary air):** the volatile gases are **completely oxidised** in the gas phase with excess air to CO₂ and H₂O.

Staging the air gives **better burnout, higher efficiency and lower emissions** (less CO, soot and NOₓ).

## Q2. Pollutants from combustion; control of unburnt pollutants, NOₓ and particulates; fuel-N → NOₓ

**Pollutants generated:**
- **Unburnt/incomplete-combustion products:** CO, unburnt hydrocarbons (CₓHᵧ), soot/tar, PAHs.
- **NOₓ** (NO, NO₂) and **N₂O**.
- **SOₓ** (mainly SO₂) and **HCl** (from S and Cl in fuel).
- **Particulate matter (fly ash, aerosols)** containing K, Na, Ca, etc.

**Control measures:**
- **Unburnt pollutants (CO, hydrocarbons, soot):** ensure complete combustion → adequate **residence time, turbulence (mixing) and temperature** ("the three T's"), supply **secondary air**, and use **staged combustion**.
- **NOₓ:** primarily **air-staging / staged combustion**, flue-gas recirculation, lower peak temperatures, and reducing conditions in the primary zone; secondary measures include **SNCR/SCR**.
- **Particulates:** gas-cleaning equipment — **cyclones, electrostatic precipitators (ESP), bag/fabric filters, scrubbers**.

**Conversion of fuel-nitrogen to NOₓ (mechanism):**
Biomass NOₓ is dominated by the **fuel-NO** route (thermal-NO is small because temperatures are moderate, ~800–1000 °C).

```
 Fuel-N  ──devolatilisation──► volatile-N (NH3, HCN)  ──+O2──►  NO
   │                                  │
   │                                  └──(reducing/staged zone)──► N2  (NO reduced)
   └──char-N ──+O2──► NO
```

Fuel-bound nitrogen is released during devolatilisation as **NH₃ and HCN** (plus char-N). Under **oxidising** conditions these are oxidised to **NO**; under **reducing/fuel-rich (staged)** conditions they are converted preferentially to harmless **N₂**. Hence **air-staging** reduces fuel-NOₓ.

## Q3. Why is global interest in biomass energy rising, and how does combustion help?

- **Depletion of fossil fuels, rising petroleum prices, growing energy demand and environmental concerns** (CO₂/climate) drive the search for renewables.
- Biomass is **renewable, abundant, evenly distributed, carbon-neutral, biodegradable** and low in ash/N/S — it can substitute fossil fuels in power plants ("stored solar energy").
- **Combustion** is the **most mature and commercially viable** biomass-to-energy technology: it directly produces heat for **power generation** over a wide capacity range, uses existing boiler/turbine infrastructure, and can handle almost any biomass with <50 % moisture. With modern **staged combustion and gas cleaning**, emissions are controlled — making it the readiest route to displace fossil energy.

## Q4. Stationary fluidized-bed combustor with staged combustion (Lurgi)

**Working principle:**
- A bed of **inert material (sand)** is **fluidised** by combustion air blown upward through a **distributor plate**, suspending the solids in a turbulent "fluid-like" state.
- Biomass fuel is fed into this hot, well-mixed bed; the large thermal inertia and excellent **heat & mass transfer** give **uniform (near-isothermal) bed temperature (~800–900 °C)** and stable combustion even of low-grade/high-moisture fuels.
- **Staged combustion (Lurgi type):** **primary air** fluidises the bed and sustains sub-stoichiometric (reducing) combustion of the solids; **secondary air** is injected **above the bed (freeboard)** to complete the oxidation of the released volatile gases.
- Benefits: low NOₓ (staging + moderate temperature), fuel flexibility, good burnout, and the ability to add sorbents (e.g., limestone) for sulphur capture.

```
   Fuel ─►┌───────────────┐  ◄─ Secondary air (freeboard, complete burnout)
          │   FREEBOARD    │ ─► flue gas out
          │~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│  ← bed surface
          │ FLUIDISED SAND │
          │   BED + FUEL   │
          └──┬─distributor─┘
             ▲
       Primary (fluidising) air
```

## Q5. Biomass combustion technologies — merits and demerits

Main systems (small → large scale):

1. **Stoves / fireplaces (domestic, small):** simple, cheap; low efficiency, high emissions.
2. **Fixed-bed / grate-fired furnaces (under-feed stoker, grate firing):** robust, handle varied fuel size/moisture, good for small–medium scale; lower efficiency, higher emissions and manual ash handling than fluidised systems.
3. **Fluidised-bed combustors (bubbling BFB / circulating CFB):** excellent mixing & heat transfer, near-isothermal, **fuel-flexible, low NOₓ with staging, in-situ S capture**; higher capital cost, sand handling, fly-ash/erosion issues.
4. **Pulverised / suspension firing & large grate boilers (large scale):** high capacity and efficiency for power; need finely sized/dry fuel and large gas-cleaning trains.
5. **Co-combustion (co-firing with coal):** uses existing plant, low extra capital; limited biomass share, fouling/slagging risk.

**General merits of combustion:** mature, commercially viable, heat directly usable for power.
**General demerits:** not inherently environment-friendly (emissions), no heat storage, and large gaseous-product volume needs **big gas-cleaning equipment**.

## Q6. Co-combustion and the growing interest in it

**Co-combustion (co-firing)** = burning **biomass together with a conventional fuel (usually coal)** in the same boiler.

**Why interest is growing:**
- Uses **existing coal-power infrastructure** with minimal modification → **low incremental capital cost** and quick deployment.
- **Reduces net CO₂, SOₓ and (often) NOₓ** emissions by displacing part of the fossil fuel with carbon-neutral biomass.
- Helps meet **renewable-energy targets** and provides an outlet for agricultural/forestry residues.
- Improves fuel-supply flexibility and can lower fuel cost.
- Achieves higher efficiency than many small stand-alone biomass plants (benefits from large-plant economies of scale).

## Q7. Recent trends in biomass combustion technology

- **Staged/low-NOₓ combustion** (air & fuel staging, flue-gas recirculation).
- **Fluidised-bed (BFB/CFB)** systems for fuel flexibility and low emissions.
- **Co-firing** of biomass with coal in large utility boilers.
- Advanced **automatic control** of the air/fuel ratio for efficiency and emission reduction.
- Improved **gas cleaning** (ESP, fabric filters, scrubbers) and **ash utilisation**.
- **Combined heat and power (CHP)** and integration with steam cycles for higher overall efficiency.
- Fuel **pre-treatment** (drying, pelletisation, torrefaction) to upgrade biomass before combustion.

## Q8. Opportunities and challenges of combustion using biomass feedstock

**Opportunities**
- Mature, reliable, commercially proven route to heat and power.
- Carbon-neutral, renewable energy; waste-to-energy and waste disposal benefit.
- Uses existing boiler/turbine technology; suitable for CHP; supports rural energy and energy-security targets.

**Challenges**
- **High/variable moisture** lowers efficiency (needs <50 % moisture or pre-drying).
- **Low energy density** and dispersed, seasonal supply → collection/storage/transport cost.
- **Ash-related problems:** slagging, fouling, corrosion (alkali metals K/Na, Cl).
- **Emissions** (NOₓ, CO, particulates) require gas cleaning.
- No heat storage; large gas volume → big cleaning equipment; capital and logistics costs.

## Q9. General correlation for minimum (stoichiometric) air to burn 1 kg of fuel

Let the fuel mass fractions be **C, H, O, N, S** (kg per kg fuel). The oxidation reactions and their O₂ demand (by mass):

| Reaction | O₂ needed |
|----------|-----------|
| C + O₂ → CO₂ | 32/12 = **2.667 kg O₂ / kg C** |
| H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O | 16/2 = **8 kg O₂ / kg H** |
| S + O₂ → SO₂ | 32/32 = **1 kg O₂ / kg S** |

Nitrogen passes through unreacted; the fuel's own oxygen is credited.

**Theoretical (stoichiometric) oxygen required:**

> **O₂(min) = 2.667 C + 8 H + S − O   (kg O₂ / kg fuel)**

Since air contains **23.3 % O₂ by mass**:

> **Minimum (stoichiometric) air = (2.667 C + 8 H + S − O) / 0.233   (kg air / kg fuel)**

(Equivalently ÷0.232 if 23.2 % is used.)

## Q10. Using the same equation to find excess air for complete combustion

In practice, more than the stoichiometric air is supplied to ensure **complete combustion**. If **e** = fractional excess (e.g., 0.20 for 20 %):

> **Actual air = (1 + e) × minimum air = (1 + e) × (2.667 C + 8 H + S − O)/0.233**

and **Excess air = e × minimum air**. The excess air appears in the flue gas as **unreacted O₂** (plus the extra N₂ carried with it), which is how excess air is verified from a flue-gas (Orsat) analysis.

## Q11. Air for complete combustion (20 % excess) + flue-gas composition — four bioresources

Using the daf ultimate analyses and **min air = (2.667C + 8H + S − O)/0.233**, then ×1.20:

| Biomass | C | H | S | O | Min air (kg/kg) | Air @ 20 % excess (kg/kg) |
|---------|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Pine chips | 0.528 | 0.061 | 0.0009 | 0.405 | 6.40 | **7.69** |
| Poplar     | 0.516 | 0.061 | 0.0002 | 0.417 | 6.21 | **7.45** |
| Sawdust    | 0.498 | 0.060 | 0.0002 | 0.437 | 5.89 | **7.06** |
| Willow     | 0.498 | 0.061 | 0.0006 | 0.434 | 5.94 | **7.12** |

**Flue-gas composition** — example for **Pine chips** (per kg daf fuel):
- CO₂ = 3.667 × C = 3.667 × 0.528 = **1.936 kg**
- H₂O = 9 × H = 9 × 0.061 = **0.549 kg**
- SO₂ = 2 × S = 2 × 0.0009 = **0.0018 kg**
- N₂ (from air, 0.767 × air) = 0.767 × 7.69 = **5.90 kg** (+ fuel N₂ ≈ 0.005 kg)
- Excess O₂ = 0.233 × (excess air) = 0.233 × (7.69 − 6.40) = **0.30 kg**

So the dominant flue-gas species are **N₂ ≈ 5.9 kg, CO₂ ≈ 1.94 kg, H₂O ≈ 0.55 kg, O₂ ≈ 0.30 kg, SO₂ ≈ trace** per kg of fuel. The same procedure (CO₂ = 3.667C, H₂O = 9H, SO₂ = 2S, N₂ = 0.767×air + fuel N₂, O₂ = 0.233×excess air) is applied to Poplar, Sawdust and Willow with their air values above.

## Q12. Rice husk — actual air and flue gas per kg (25 % excess, MW air = MW flue gas = 29)

**Ultimate analysis (mass fractions):** C 0.39, H₂ 0.05, O₂ 0.327, S 0.001, N₂ 0.02, H₂O 0.036, ash 0.176.

**Step 1 — Theoretical O₂:**
O₂(min) = 2.667(0.39) + 8(0.05) + 1(0.001) − 0.327
= 1.0401 + 0.400 + 0.001 − 0.327 = **1.1141 kg O₂/kg**

**Step 2 — Theoretical air:**
= 1.1141 / 0.233 = **4.782 kg air/kg fuel**

**Step 3 — Actual air (25 % excess):**
= 1.25 × 4.782 = **5.98 kg air/kg fuel** ≈ 5.977 kg/kg

**Step 4 — Flue gas produced (overall mass balance):**
Mass in = fuel (1 kg) + air (5.977) = 6.977 kg; the **ash (0.176 kg)** leaves as solid residue, so:
Flue gas = 6.977 − 0.176 = **≈ 6.80 kg flue gas/kg fuel**

**(In molar terms, since MW = 29 for both:** actual air ≈ 5.98/29 = 0.206 kmol; flue gas ≈ 6.80/29 = 0.235 kmol per kg fuel.)

**Answer: ≈ 5.98 kg air supplied and ≈ 6.80 kg flue gas produced per kg of rice husk.**
