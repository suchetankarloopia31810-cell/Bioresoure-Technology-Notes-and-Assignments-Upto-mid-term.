## Q1. Gasification — chemical reactions and steps

**Gasification** converts solid biomass into a combustible gaseous fuel called **producer gas** through a sequence of thermochemical reactions, by **partial oxidation at high temperature (≈ 800–900 °C)**. The gas (CV ≈ 1000–1200 kcal/m³, or 4–6 MJ/Nm³) contains **CO, H₂, CO₂, CH₄ and N₂**, and can be burnt directly or used as **syngas** for chemicals (methanol, H₂).

**Main chemical reactions:**

| Reaction | Equation | ΔH (kJ/mol) |
|----------|----------|------------:|
| Partial oxidation | C + ½O₂ ↔ CO | −268 |
| Complete oxidation | C + O₂ ↔ CO₂ | −406 |
| Water-gas reaction | C + H₂O ↔ CO + H₂ | +118 |
| Boudouard reaction | C + CO₂ ↔ 2CO | +170.7 |
| Water-gas shift | CO + H₂O ↔ CO₂ + H₂ | −42 |
| Methanation | CO + 3H₂ ↔ CH₄ + H₂O | −88 |

Complete + partial oxidation (exothermic) supply the heat (≈ 60 % from complete oxidation) for the endothermic water-gas and Boudouard reactions.

**Four steps / stages (in sequence):**
1. **Drying** (endothermic) – evaporation of moisture; complete by ~150 °C.
2. **Pyrolysis** (endothermic) – thermochemical decomposition (250–700 °C) into char (solid), tars (liquid) and pyrolysis gas.
3. **Oxidation/Combustion** (exothermic) – partial oxidation of char/volatiles with limited O₂ → provides process heat; products CO, CO₂, H₂O.
4. **Reduction** (endothermic) – char + gas mixture react (water-gas, Boudouard, etc.) → final producer gas.

Process is supported by **upstream processing** (size reduction, drying, densification), **gasification**, and **downstream processing** (gas cleaning/purification).

## Q2. Gasifiers — updraft, downdraft and fluidized-bed

A **gasifier** is the reactor in which gasification takes place. Selection depends on end-use and the quantity/quality of producer gas needed. Fixed-bed (fuel stationary) types are **downdraft** and **updraft**.

**(a) Downdraft (co-current) gasifier**
- Fuel fed near the **top**; air introduced with fuel or at an intermediate level; **gas + char + ash exit the bottom**.
- Zones top→bottom: **drying → pyrolysis → combustion → reduction**.
- Pyrolysis gases pass **down through the hot combustion zone**, so **tars are cracked → low-tar, cleaner gas** (best for engines).
- Combustion zone reaches **1000–1200 °C**; often has a **throat** to promote heat/mass transfer.

```
   Fuel + air ─►┌───────────┐
                │  Drying    │
                │  Pyrolysis │
                │  Combustion│ (throat)
                │  Reduction │
                └─────┬─────┘
              gas + char/ash ▼  (clean, low-tar gas)
```

**(b) Updraft (counter-current) gasifier**
- Air injected near the **bottom** (combustion zone); **producer gas flows upward** and exits the **top** where fuel enters.
- Gas exits at **low temperature (130–150 °C)**, giving **high thermal efficiency**, but is **heavily contaminated with tar/oil/particulate** from incoming fuel.
- Solid order: drying (top) → pyrolysis → reduction → combustion (bottom); ash removed at the bottom.

**(c) Fluidized-bed gasifier**
- Uses an **inert medium (sand)**; air injected through a **distributor plate** fluidises the bed (superficial velocity 0.5–1.0 m/s).
- Turbulent mixing → **high heat transfer, near-isothermal bed at 800–900 °C**, good fuel flexibility.
- Lower temperature + low residence time → **higher tar/oil and light-hydrocarbon yields** than downdraft.

## Q3. Equivalence ratio and energy efficiency of a gasifier

**Equivalence ratio (ER, Φ):**

> **Φ = (Actual air supplied for combustion) / (Theoretical / stoichiometric air required for combustion)**

- Typical gasifier range: **Φ ≈ 0.2–0.4** (sub-stoichiometric — that is what distinguishes gasification from combustion).
- **Significance:** increasing ER **raises temperature and carbon conversion and reduces tar**, but **lowers the gas heating value** (less H₂ and CO, more CO₂). H₂ rises until Φ ≈ 0.4, then falls. So ER is the key control parameter balancing gas quality vs. tar/conversion.

**Energy (cold-gas) efficiency:**

> **η = (Chemical energy output in the dry producer gas at 15 °C) / (Energy input from the biomass)**

- **Significance:** it measures how much of the biomass chemical energy is retained in the usable gas — the main performance index of a gasifier.

## Q4. Zonal reactions (mechanisms) in co-current vs counter-current gasifiers

Both have the four zones **drying, pyrolysis, oxidation (combustion) and reduction**, but their **order relative to gas flow differs**:

**Co-current (downdraft):** gas flows in the **same direction** as the solids (downward).
- Drying (top, ~150 °C) → Pyrolysis (250–700 °C, → char + tar + gas) → Combustion (1000–1200 °C, C + O₂ → CO₂, partial → CO, gives heat) → Reduction (C + CO₂ → 2CO; C + H₂O → CO + H₂).
- Because pyrolysis tars pass **through** the high-temperature combustion/reduction zone, they are **cracked** → **low-tar product gas**.

**Counter-current (updraft):** gas flows **opposite** to the solids (gas up, solids down).
- From the top (gas exit) downward in the solid: Drying → Pyrolysis → Reduction → Combustion (bottom, where air enters); ash at the bottom.
- Hot gas rises and dries the incoming fuel (high efficiency, low exit temperature 130–150 °C) **but the freshly released tars leave with the gas without cracking** → **high-tar product gas**.

**Products:** both give CO, H₂, CO₂, CH₄, N₂ (with air); downdraft → clean low-tar gas; updraft → tar/oil-rich gas.

## Q5. Advantages/disadvantages of gasification + biomass factors affecting it

**Advantages of gasification**
- Very **low emissions**; better process control (lower temperature than combustion).
- Produces a **small volume of gas** → less cleaning equipment needed.
- Produces **char** that can be used after activation.
- Versatile gas — heat, power (gas engines/turbines, BIG/CC), or **syngas** for chemicals/H₂.

**Disadvantages**
- **High capital cost**; complex operation (oxygen-separation units).
- Economics sensitive to **steam and oxygen** costs.
- **High-ash feedstock → agglomeration** problems.

**Desirable biomass properties / factors affecting gasification**
- **Equivalence ratio (ER):** optimum 0.2–0.4.
- **Moisture content:** low preferred (downdraft max ~40 % dry basis; updraft tolerates more); high moisture lowers conversion.
- **Biomass characteristics:** density, particle size; VM, FC, ash, calorific value; ultimate (C,H,N,S) analysis.
- **Operating temperature:** >800 °C for high conversion & low tar (keep <750 °C to avoid ash agglomeration).
- **Gasifying agent:** air/steam/oxygen/CO₂ — steam ↑ heating value, air ↓ heating value (N₂ dilution).
- **Superficial velocity, residence time, pressure, catalyst.**

## Q6. Design considerations for fixed-bed stationary gasifiers

- **Feedstock preparation / upstream processing:** size reduction & screening, drying to suitable moisture, densification (low biomass density), removal of magnetic materials.
- **Choice of configuration:** downdraft (clean, low-tar gas for engines) vs updraft (high efficiency, tar-rich gas for direct heat).
- **Zonal arrangement:** provide distinct drying, pyrolysis, combustion and reduction zones; in downdraft a **throat (restriction)** increases gas velocity and promotes heat/mass transfer and tar cracking.
- **Air/oxidant injection & equivalence ratio:** maintain ER ≈ 0.2–0.4; locate air entry to control combustion-zone temperature (1000–1200 °C downdraft).
- **Temperature control:** keep combustion temperature high enough for conversion/tar cracking yet below the **ash-agglomeration/slagging** limit (~750 °C in the bed where relevant).
- **Ash & char removal:** grate / ash-extraction at the bottom; handle slagging, fouling, corrosion from inorganics.
- **Gas exit & downstream cleaning:** slight vacuum at exit; cyclones/filters/scrubbers for tar and particulate removal.
- **Residence time and bed height** sized for complete reduction; **materials** chosen for high-temperature, corrosive service.

## Q7. What is pyrolysis? (with schematic)

**Pyrolysis** is the **thermochemical decomposition of biomass by heating in the absence of (or with very limited) air/oxygen**, typically around **500 °C**, producing three fractions:
- **Liquid – bio-oil / bio-crude**
- **Solid – biochar**
- **Gas – pyrolysis gas / syngas**

Flash/fast pyrolysis maximises **bio-oil** (up to ~80 % efficiency); slow pyrolysis favours **char**.

```
                 Heat (no/low O2, ~500 °C)
   BIOMASS  ───────────────────────────────►  ┌── Bio-oil (liquid)
   (dried,                                     ├── Bio-char (solid)
   ground)                                     └── Pyro-gas/syngas (CO, H2, CH4...)
```

## Q8. Feedstocks for pyrolysis + mechanism

**Possible feedstocks:** lignocellulosic biomass — wood and wood wastes, agricultural residues (rice husk, straw, bagasse, corncob, nut shells), forestry residues, energy crops, MSW organic fraction, and other carbonaceous wastes.

**Mechanism (3 broad routes / stages):**
1. **Primary decomposition:** on heating, the biomass polymers (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) crack — chemical bonds break to form **smaller molecules** → primary vapours, char and gases.
2. **Vapour-phase reactions:** primary vapours can be **condensed** to bio-oil, or undergo **secondary cracking** at higher temperature → more non-condensable gases (CO, H₂, CH₄) and a little extra char.
3. **Product split depends on conditions:** low T + long residence + slow heating → **char**; high T + fast heating + short residence (flash/fast) → **bio-oil**; very high T favours **gas** (tar cracking).

## Q9. Pyrolysis products and their applications

1. **Bio-oil (bio-crude, liquid):** can be used in engines, turbines and boilers, or as a refinery feedstock; needs **upgrading** (deoxygenation, removing alkalis by hydrogenation/catalytic cracking) because it has **low heating value, is corrosive, thermally unstable and immiscible with hydrocarbons**.
2. **Biochar (solid):** soil amendment / fertiliser carrier in **agriculture** (improves fertility, water retention, carbon sequestration); activated to **activated carbon**; solid fuel.
3. **Pyrolysis gas / syngas (≈10–35 % in slow pyrolysis):** mainly **H₂ + CO** (also CH₄, C₂H₄, C₂H₆, CO₂, N₂); used as fuel in **industrial combustion and IC engines** (converted petrol/diesel engines); higher T favours gas yield via tar cracking.

## Q10. Short note on syngas

**Syngas (synthesis gas)** is the gaseous product consisting mainly of **hydrogen (H₂) and carbon monoxide (CO)**, with minor **CH₄, CO₂, N₂, water, light hydrocarbons (C₂H₄, C₂H₆), tar and ash** depending on feedstock and conditions. It is produced by **gasification** and by **pyrolysis** (the pyro-gas is also called syngas). Uses: **fuel** for gas engines/turbines and industrial combustion (power/heat), and a **chemical feedstock** for producing **methanol, hydrogen and synthetic fuels**. Higher temperatures (tar cracking) increase syngas yield and quality.

## Q11. Types of pyrolysers; fixed-bed vs fluidized-bed; comparison

**Types of pyrolysers:** fixed-bed, **fluidized-bed** (bubbling & circulating), **ablative**, **vacuum**, **rotating-cone**, and **auger (screw)** reactors.

**Fixed-bed reactor:** simple, biomass held stationary on a bed/grate; low heating rate, long residence time → favours **char (slow pyrolysis)**; simple and cheap but **non-uniform heating, lower bio-oil yield, poor for large scale**.

**Fluidized-bed reactor (bubbling/circulating):** uses an inert solid (sand) fluidised by gas; gives **rapid heat transfer, large fluid–solid contact area, uniform temperature** → **maximises bio-oil (fast pyrolysis)**; well-proven and widely used industrially, easily scaled; but needs fine particles, inert gas and a fluidising system.

**Comparison (overall performance/efficiency):**

| Feature | Fixed-bed | Fluidized-bed |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| Heating rate | Low | High |
| Heat/mass transfer | Poor–moderate | Excellent |
| Temperature uniformity | Non-uniform | Near-isothermal |
| Main product | Char (slow) | Bio-oil (fast) |
| Scale-up | Limited | Good, commercial |
| Complexity/cost | Low | Higher (fluidising gas, sand) |
| Bio-oil yield/quality | Lower | Higher |

## Q12. Current status and future challenges of pyrolysis technology

**Current status**
- Driven by fossil-fuel depletion, food-security and environmental concerns; helps **cut CO₂** (bio-oils derive from CO₂-absorbing plants).
- **Fast pyrolysis** (high heating rate, short residence) → high-quality **bio-oil**; **slow pyrolysis** (moderate T, long residence) → **biochar**; intermediate pyrolysis balances both.
- Main present interests: **CO₂ mitigation, electricity generation, energy independence**; an **ablative reactor** can convert whole wood chips with more energy.

**Future challenges**
- Lack of **markets for bio-oil** and standardised **biochar products**.
- Need **flexible reactor designs**, **catalysts for bio-oil upgrading**, **deoxygenation in the gas phase**, and better feedstock logistics & heating rates.
- Reduce **metal/ash accumulation** in the bed (smaller particles, controlled T & heating rate).
- Integrate pyrolysis with **gasification/bio-refineries**; speed up commercialisation and **bio-oil refineries**.
- Selection of technology/feedstock/parameters should be based on **economic trade-offs**.

## Q13. Anaerobic digestion — pathways of organic-matter degradation

**Anaerobic digestion (AD)** is the conversion of organic material (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) **to biogas and digestate in the absence of oxygen**, by a series of metabolic interactions among microbial groups. It occurs in **four stages**:

```
 Complex organics (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids)
        │  (1) HYDROLYSIS  — hydrolytic enzymes
        ▼
 Sugars, amino acids, fatty acids
        │  (2) ACIDOGENESIS — fermentative bacteria
        ▼
 VFAs (propionic, butyric, acetic), alcohols, H2, CO2
        │  (3) ACETOGENESIS — acetogenic bacteria
        ▼
 Acetate (CH3COOH) + H2 + CO2
        │  (4) METHANOGENESIS — methanogens
        ▼
 BIOGAS  (CH4 + CO2)
```

1. **Hydrolysis/liquefaction:** insoluble polymers broken into soluble monomers by hydrolytic enzymes — lipids→fatty acids, polysaccharides→monosaccharides (cellulose→glucose), proteins→amino acids, nucleic acids→purines/pyrimidines. Often **rate-limiting** for high-solid wastes.
2. **Acidogenesis (fermentation):** sugars/amino acids/fatty acids → **VFAs (propionic, butyric, acetic), alcohols, H₂, CO₂** by facultative/anaerobic bacteria. e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆ + 2H₂O → 2CH₃COOH + 2CO₂ + 4H₂.
3. **Acetogenesis:** VFAs (>2 C), alcohols → **acetate + H₂ + CO₂** by obligate H₂-producing acetogens (e.g. *Syntrophobacter*, *Syntrophomonas*); homoacetogens make acetate from CO₂+H₂.
4. **Methanogenesis:** methanogens (*Methanobacterium*, *Methanococcus*, *Methanosarcina*, *Methanosaeta*) produce CH₄:
   - **Aceticlastic:** CH₃COOH → CH₄ + CO₂ (≈70 % of methane)
   - **Hydrogenotrophic:** CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O (≈30 %)

## Q14. Factors affecting anaerobic digestion

- **Temperature:** works 8–55 °C; **optimum ≈ 35 °C** (mesophilic); very slow at 8 °C.
- **pH:** **6.8–7.8** optimum; above 8.5 bacteria cannot survive.
- **C/N ratio:** methanogens consume C ~30–35× faster than N → **optimum C:N ≈ 30:1**; strictly anaerobic (no O₂).
- **Solids/water proportion:** digestion proceeds well at **8–9 % solids**; raw gobar (~18–20 % solids) is **diluted 1:1 with water**.
- **Retention time (RT):** = reactor volume ÷ daily feed volume (e.g., 20 L reactor fed 4 L/day → RT = 5 days).
- **Volumetric organic loading rate:** organic matter added per day per reactor volume; *Loading rate (%) = (% organic matter in feed) / RT*.
- **Nutrients / trace metals:** Ni, Co, Mo, Fe (also Se, Mn, Al, B) stimulate methanogenesis; **Ni requirement is unique to methanogens**.

## Q15. Products of AD; advantages/disadvantages of AD and co-digestion

**Products:** **Biogas** (CH₄ 50–70 %, CO₂ 30–40 %, trace H₂S etc.) and **digestate** (nutrient-rich residue used as biofertiliser).

**Advantages of AD**
- Renewable energy (biogas) **+ waste treatment** simultaneously; ~90 % of substrate energy transferred to easily purifiable biogas.
- Reduces pollution/odour/pathogens; produces **digestate fertiliser**; works on wet/high-moisture wastes.

**Disadvantages of AD**
- **Slow** process; sensitive to T, pH, toxic/inhibitory compounds (NH₃, H₂S from amino-acid fermentation).
- Hydrolysis can be rate-limiting; needs careful control; biogas needs cleaning before some uses.

**Co-digestion** (digesting two or more substrates together, e.g., manure + crop/food waste):
- **Advantages:** balances C:N ratio and nutrients, dilutes inhibitors, **higher biogas yield & stability**, better use of digester capacity.
- **Disadvantages:** needs feedstock-mix control and collection logistics; risk of imbalance/inhibition if mix is wrong.

## Q16. Biogasification and the Indian scenario (cow dung)

**Biogasification** = **anaerobic degradation/fermentation of organic matter (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) to biogas.** Biogas is a colourless, flammable mixture (CH₄ 50–70 %, CO₂ 30–40 %, traces) from anaerobic digestion of plant/animal organic wastes. ~90 % of the substrate energy is transferred to the biogas.

**Indian scenario (cow dung):**
- Cow dung = undigested residue excreted by bovines; **faeces:urine ≈ 3:1**; mainly lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose + ~24 minerals (N, K, S, Fe, Mg, Cu, Co, Mn).
- ~**69.9 % of India's population is rural**, where the cow (*Bos indicus*) is the major cattle, generating **9–15 kg dung/day**; indigenous cows have higher Ca, P, Zn, Cu than cross-breeds.
- Cow dung is a **cheap, abundant bioresource** with rich microbial diversity (*Bacillus*, *Corynebacterium*, *Lactobacillus*, yeasts, etc.).
- Traditional uses: **direct burning as fuel, wall/floor plastering (insulation), smoke as mosquito repellent, ash as cleaning agent, manure** — high socio-economic value in the village economy.
- Biogasification serves the **dual purpose**: alternative energy + waste management, easing **fuel scarcity and sewage-disposal** problems.

## Q17. Indian floating-dome (gobar gas) plant — diagram and description

**KVIC (Khadi & Village Industries Commission) floating-dome model:**

```
   Inlet pit        ┌───── Gas outlet (nipple + valve)
   (raw slurry)     │   ┌──────────┐
        │           ▼   │ Floating  │  ← G.I. gas holder
        ▼     ┌─────────┤ gas dome  ├─────────┐
   ====gas====│   gas    └──────────┘  gas    │====
   ───────────┤========================────────┤───  slurry level
              │   Slurry (dividing wall to     │
              │   half height) — DIGESTER      │── Outlet pit
              │   (underground brick tank)     │   (spent slurry/
              └───────────┬───────────────────┘    digestate)
                     centre wall + MS support
```

**Description:**
- A **mortar/cement-lined brick cylindrical tank built underground** is the digester, divided into **two halves by a wall extending to half the height**.
- A **MS structure on the centre wall supports a cylindrical G.I. gas holder (floating dome)**; gas is drawn through a **nipple with valve**; the clearance between holder and wall is **sealed with oil/water**.
- Two ground-level pits — **inlet (raw gobar slurry)** and **outlet (digestate withdrawal)** — connect to the digester by pipes.
- The digester is filled with slurry up to the discharge-pit level; after **15–20 days** biogas starts collecting in the holder (which **rises/floats** as gas accumulates), and can be drawn after testing. (India's first cattle-dung biogas plant was patented in **1946**.)

## Q19. Short note on biogas + recent developments

**Biogas:** a colourless, flammable gas mixture from anaerobic digestion — **CH₄ 50–70 %, CO₂ 30–40 %**, plus traces (H₂S, N₂, H₂). It is a clean, renewable fuel for **cooking, lighting, heating and power generation**, and the leftover **digestate is a good biofertiliser**. It tackles both **energy shortage and waste disposal**.

**Recent developments:**
- **Biogas upgrading** to **bio-methane / bio-CNG** (CO₂ and H₂S removal) for vehicle fuel and grid injection.
- **Co-digestion** of cow dung with crop/food/municipal waste for higher yield.
- Improved digester designs (fixed-dome, floating-dome, plug-flow, high-rate reactors) and **trace-metal/additive supplementation** to boost methanogenesis.
- Use of cow-dung **microorganisms for biofuel production and pollutant management**; integration with rural energy and waste-management programmes.

## Q20. Design of a gobar gas plant (dairy farm) — full numerical

**Given:**
- 50 grown cows + 40 grown buffaloes (calves' dung not separately specified — taken as already included / neglected; method shown for the grown animals).
- Gobar/cow/day = 10 kg; Gobar/buffalo/day = 15 kg
- Biogas/kg gobar/day = 0.035 m³
- Solids in gobar = 20 %; bulk density ρ_G = 1200 kg/m³
- Max digester capacity (small plant) = 60 m³; water:gobar = 1:1

**Step 1 — Daily gobar:**
= 50 × 10 + 40 × 15 = 500 + 600 = **1100 kg/day**

**Step 2 — Daily biogas:**
= 1100 × 0.035 = **38.5 m³/day**

**Step 3 — Volume of gobar/day:**
= 1100 / 1200 = **0.917 m³/day**

**Step 4 — Slurry volume/day (1:1 water:gobar):**
= 0.917 + 0.917 = **1.83 m³/day**
(Dilution halves solids: 20 % → **10 %**, near the 8–9 % optimum.)

**Step 5 — Digester volume (retention time = 30 days):**
V = 30 × 1.83 = **55 m³**  (< 60 m³ max → a single small plant is feasible)

**Step 6 — Digester dimensions** (V = (π/4)D²h). Take slurry height h = 5 m:
D² = 4V/(πh) = (4 × 55)/(π × 5) = 14.0 → **D ≈ 3.74 m**
Add ~0.5 m free space above slurry → **total height H ≈ 5.5 m**

**Step 7 — Gas holder** (store ~50 % of daily gas):
V_G = 38.5 / 2 ≈ **19.25 m³**
Clearance 0.05 m → D_G = D − 0.1 = 3.74 − 0.1 = **3.64 m**
h_G = 4V_G/(πD_G²) = (4 × 19.25)/(π × 3.64²) ≈ **1.85 m** (+0.03 m dip → **H_G ≈ 1.88 m**)

**Step 8 — Slurry mixing tank** (= daily slurry volume = 1.83 m³):
Take 1.5 m × 1.5 m → height = 1.83/2.25 ≈ **0.81 m**, i.e. tank ≈ **1.5 × 1.5 × 0.8 m**.

**Design summary**

| Item | Result |
|------|--------|
| Daily gobar | 1100 kg/day |
| Daily biogas | 38.5 m³/day |
| Slurry/day | 1.83 m³/day |
| Digester | V = 55 m³, D ≈ 3.74 m, H ≈ 5.5 m |
| Gas holder | V_G ≈ 19.25 m³, D_G ≈ 3.64 m, H_G ≈ 1.88 m |
| Mixing tank | ≈ 1.5 × 1.5 × 0.8 m |

*(Method follows the worked KVIC floating-dome example in Lecture-11. If your instructor wants calves counted as half an adult of the same type, add 50 cow-calves × 5 kg + 40 buffalo-calves × 7.5 kg = 550 kg/day to the dung, giving 1650 kg/day, and repeat Steps 2–8 with two digesters since V would exceed the 60 m³ small-plant limit.)*
